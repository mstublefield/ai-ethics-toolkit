# Mental Health

> AI anxiety is real, widespread, and under-discussed. In practitioner communities it's the single most commonly raised concern — more than any technical ethics issue.

## What this principle covers

The mental-health dimension of AI ethics covers the effect of AI on the person making the decision: the fear of obsolescence, the guilt of participating in displacement, the overwhelm of keeping up, the exhaustion of having to have an opinion on everything. It's not soft; it directly shapes what decisions get made and how honestly.

## Why it belongs in an ethics skill

Two reasons:

1. **A burned-out practitioner makes worse ethical decisions.** Anxiety pushes toward shortcuts, toward "fine, whatever," toward unthinking defaults. Tired people skip the Five Questions.
2. **The field's mental-health load shapes its collective choices.** When everyone is defensive, the conversation defaults to hype or doom, neither of which produces good policy.

## What this doesn't mean

It doesn't mean "take care of yourself, have a bubble bath." It means: recognise that decisions are made by people, and people have a finite capacity to absorb uncertainty. Structural choices — slower news diet, fewer tools to evaluate at once, a peer to talk to — are ethical tools too.

## Practical moves

- When a client pushes for faster or cheaper AI-enabled work than feels right, name the dynamic. Often they don't actually want the cheaper version; they want to be reassured they're not being ripped off.
- When you notice AI use starting to feel obligatory rather than chosen, stop. Unthinking use is where most ethical drift begins.
- Permission to not have an opinion on every new tool. It's OK to let something sit for a month before taking a position.

## Not a rule, a reminder

This principle doesn't belong in most external AI use policies — clients generally don't need your philosophy on burnout. But it belongs in the internal version, because the internal version is how you survive producing the external version honestly.
